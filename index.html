Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle

Δx · Δp ≥ ℏ/2
Problem 1.1 — Electron Localization Calculation

An electron is localized within a region of Δx = 250 picometers. What is the minimum uncertainty in its velocity, Δvmin, in m/s? Report to two significant figures.
(me = 9.109 × 10−31 kg, ℏ = 1.0546 × 10−34 J·s)

Δvmin = ℏ / (2 · m · Δx)
Convert picometers to meters first: 250 pm = 250 × 10−12 m = 2.50 × 10−10 m. Then plug directly into the formula.

Step-by-Step Solution

Δx = 250 × 10−12 m = 2.50 × 10−10 m
Δv = ℏ / (2 · me · Δx) = (1.0546 × 10−34) / (2 × 9.109 × 10−31 × 2.50 × 10−10)
Denominator = 4.555 × 10−40 kg·m
Δv = 1.0546 × 10−34 / 4.555 × 10−40 = 2.315 × 105 m/s
Δvmin ≈ 2.3 × 10⁵ m/s
⚠️ This is enormous — nearly 0.08% the speed of light! Uncertainty is immense for subatomic particles.
Problem 1.2 — Electron vs. Bowling Ball Comparison

Both an electron (m = 9.109 × 10−31 kg) and a bowling ball (m = 5.0 kg) are confined to a position uncertainty of Δx = 1.0 nm. Calculate Δvmin for each. What does the comparison reveal?

Same formula, different masses. Note how much heavier the bowling ball is — mass is in the denominator, so a bigger mass means a smaller (and practically immeasurable) velocity uncertainty.

Step-by-Step Solution

Δx = 1.0 × 10−9 m for both
Electron: Δv = 1.0546×10−34 / (2 × 9.109×10−31 × 1.0×10−9) = 5.8 × 104 m/s
Bowling ball: Δv = 1.0546×10−34 / (2 × 5.0 × 1.0×10−9) = 1.1 × 10−26 m/s — effectively zero!
Electron: ~5.8 × 10⁴ m/s (huge!) | Bowling ball: ~10⁻²⁶ m/s (unmeasurable)
Quantum uncertainty matters only at atomic/subatomic scales. For everyday objects, it is completely negligible.

Quantum Numbers (n, ℓ, mℓ, ms)

ℓ: 0→n−1 | mℓ: −ℓ→+ℓ | ms: ±½
Problem 2.1 — Valid or Invalid? Conceptual

For each set of quantum numbers, state VALID or INVALID and explain why if invalid:

#nℓmℓms
a330+½
b21−1−½
c401+½
d53−2+½
e3210
Three rules: (1) ℓ must be 0, 1, 2 … up to n−1. (2) mℓ must be an integer from −ℓ to +ℓ. (3) ms can only be +½ or −½.

Answers

a) INVALID — When n = 3, ℓ can only be 0, 1, or 2. ℓ = 3 is not allowed.
b) VALID — n=2, ℓ=1 (p orbital ✓), mℓ=−1 ✓ (within −1 to +1), ms=−½ ✓
c) INVALID — When ℓ = 0, the only allowed value is mℓ = 0. mℓ = 1 is not permitted.
d) VALID — n=5, ℓ=3 (f orbital ✓ since ℓ ≤ 4), mℓ=−2 ✓, ms=+½ ✓
e) INVALID — ms = 0 is not allowed. Spin must be exactly +½ or −½.
Problem 2.2 — The 4f Subshell Enumeration

For the 4f subshell: (a) What are all allowed values of mℓ? (b) How many orbitals are in this subshell? (c) What is the maximum number of electrons it can hold?

Answers

4f: n = 4, ℓ = 3
(a) mℓ = −3, −2, −1, 0, +1, +2, +3  (seven values)
(b) # orbitals = 2ℓ + 1 = 2(3) + 1 = 7 orbitals
(c) Max electrons = 7 × 2 = 14 electrons
4f: 7 orbitals, max 14 electrons

Photoelectric Effect & Work Function

KE = hν − Φ | v = √(2KE/mₑ)
Problem 3.1 — Find Electron Speed from λ and Φ Multi-step

A metal has a work function Φ = 2.20 eV. Light of wavelength 410 nm strikes the surface. (a) What is the frequency of the light? (b) What is the photon energy in eV? (c) What is the kinetic energy of the ejected electron in eV? (d) What is the electron's speed?
(me = 9.109 × 10−31 kg, 1 eV = 1.602 × 10−19 J)

Ephoton = hc/λ  |  KE = Ephoton − Φ  |  v = √(2·KE/me)
Three formulas in sequence — work left to right: λ → ν → Ephoton(eV) → KE → v. Convert eV ↔ J carefully at each step!

Step-by-Step Solution

(a) ν = c/λ = (3.00×108 m/s) / (410×10−9 m) = 7.32 × 1014 Hz
(b) Ephoton = hν = (6.626×10−34)(7.32×1014) = 4.85×10−19 J ÷ 1.602×10−19 = 3.03 eV
(c) KE = 3.03 − 2.20 = 0.83 eV = 0.83 × 1.602×10−19 = 1.33 × 10−19 J
(d) v = √(2 × 1.33×10−19 / 9.109×10−31) = √(2.92×1011) = 5.4 × 105 m/s
Electron speed ≈ 5.4 × 10⁵ m/s
Problem 3.2 — Find the Work Function ⭐ Direct Exam Problem

Light of wavelength 532.0 nm hits a clean metal surface. The fastest ejected electrons have a speed of 3.41 × 105 m/s.
(a) Find the frequency ν of the light in Hz. (b) Find the photon energy in eV. (c) Find the work function Φ in eV.

Φ = Ephoton − ½mev²
This is the reverse of Problem 3.1 — you know the electron's speed, so calculate KE first (½mv²), then subtract it from Ephoton to find Φ.

Step-by-Step Solution

(a) ν = c/λ = 3.00×108 / 532.0×10−9 = 5.639 × 1014 Hz
(b) Ephoton = hν = 6.626×10−34 × 5.639×1014 = 3.736×10−19 J ÷ 1.602×10−19 = 2.33 eV
(c) KE = ½mev² = ½ × 9.109×10−31 × (3.41×105)2 = 5.30×10−20 J = 0.331 eV
Φ = 2.33 − 0.33 = 2.00 eV
Φ ≈ 2.00 eV

Hydrogen Atom Energy Transitions

Eₙ = −13.598 eV / n²
Problem 4.1 — Balmer Series: n=6 → n=2 ⭐ 45% Missed on Exam

A hydrogen electron transitions from n = 6 to n = 2. (a) Calculate the energy of the emitted photon in eV. (b) Calculate the wavelength λ in nm. (c) Is this UV, visible, or IR?
Use En = −2.18 × 10−18 J × (1/n²).

ΔE = Efinal − Einitial  |  λ = hc / |ΔE|
Calculate E6 and E2 separately, then ΔE = E2 − E6. A negative ΔE means emission (energy leaves the atom). Use |ΔE| in the wavelength formula.

Step-by-Step Solution

E6 = −2.18×10−18 × (1/36) = −6.056 × 10−20 J
E2 = −2.18×10−18 × (1/4) = −5.45 × 10−19 J
ΔE = E2 − E6 = −5.45×10−19 − (−6.056×10−20) = −4.844 × 10−19 J  (negative → emission ✓)
λ = hc/|ΔE| = (1.988×10−25) / (4.844×10−19) = 4.10×10−7 m = 410 nm
410 nm is visible violet light (Balmer series — all transitions ending at n=2 are visible).
λ ≈ 410 nm — visible violet (Balmer series)
Problem 4.2 — 3p → 1s Emission in eV Calculation

A hydrogen atom has its electron in a 3p orbital. It transitions to 1s and emits light. (a) Calculate the emitted photon energy in eV to three significant digits. (b) Find the wavelength in nm.
Use En = −13.598 eV / n².

Ephoton = |E1 − E3|. Calculate each level, subtract, and take the absolute value — that's the energy of the emitted photon. Note: the orbital label (3p) doesn't change the energy in hydrogen; only n matters.

Step-by-Step Solution

E1 = −13.598 / 1² = −13.598 eV
E3 = −13.598 / 3² = −13.598 / 9 = −1.511 eV
Ephoton = |E1 − E3| = |−13.598 − (−1.511)| = 12.1 eV
Convert to J: 12.087 × 1.602×10−19 = 1.937×10−18 J
λ = 1.988×10−25 / 1.937×10−18 = 1.026×10−7 m = 102.6 nm (UV — Lyman series)
Ephoton = 12.1 eV  |  λ ≈ 103 nm (UV)
Problem 4.3 — Ionization Wavelength from Ground State Conceptual + Calc

A hydrogen atom is in the ground state (n = 1, ℓ = 0). What is the longest-wavelength photon that could ionize the atom (promote the electron to n → ∞)? Report λ in nm to three significant figures.

At n = ∞, E∞ = 0. So the required photon energy equals exactly |E1|. "Longest wavelength" means minimum photon energy — just barely enough to ionize.

Step-by-Step Solution

E∞ = 0  |  E1 = −13.598 eV
Required energy = 0 − (−13.598) = 13.598 eV = 13.598 × 1.602×10−19 = 2.179 × 10−18 J
λ = hc/E = 1.988×10−25 / 2.179×10−18 = 9.12 × 10−8 m = 91.2 nm
This is the Lyman series limit — extreme ultraviolet (far UV).
λionization = 91.2 nm (extreme UV)

Problem 5.2 — Orbital Descriptions + Nodal Planes of 3dxy ⭐ Direct Exam Question

For each orbital: describe the shape, state the number of each type of node, and identify any nodal planes.
Orbitals: 3s  |  2py  |  3dxy  |  3dz²

The subscript tells you orientation (py points along y; dxy has lobes between x and y axes). The ± phase signs on lobes are just the math signs of ψ — not charges! Angular nodes for d orbitals (ℓ=2) are planes, except dz² which has conical nodes.

Orbital Descriptions

3s: Spherical. 2 radial nodes (two concentric spherical shells), 0 nodal planes. Three concentric regions of alternating wavefunction phase (+/−/+).
2py: Dumbbell shape with two lobes oriented along the y-axis. 0 radial nodes, 1 nodal plane — the xz-plane. Upper and lower lobes have opposite phase signs.
3dxy: Four-lobed cloverleaf. Lobes point between the x and y axes (at 45°). 0 radial nodes, 2 nodal planes — the xz-plane and the yz-plane. Lobes alternate in phase.
3dz²: Unique: two large lobes along the z-axis plus a torus (donut) ring in the xy-plane. 0 radial nodes, 2 angular nodes — but they are conical nodes, not flat planes.
3dxy has 2 nodal planes: the xz-plane and the yz-plane
💡 When drawing orbitals on x-y axes: ± signs show phase, not charge. Always label which axis each lobe points along or between!

Wien's Displacement Law (λmax)

λmax · T = 2.898 × 10⁻³ m·K
Problem 6.1 — Earth's Peak Emission Calculation

Earth's average surface temperature is approximately 288 K. Calculate Earth's peak thermal-emission wavelength, λmax, in μm. Report to two significant figures.

λmax = (2.898 × 10⁻³ m·K) / T
Plug in T = 288 K directly. Then convert meters to micrometers: 1 m = 106 μm.

Step-by-Step Solution

λmax = 2.898×10−3 m·K / 288 K = 1.007 × 10−5 m
Convert: 1.007×10−5 m × 106 μm/m = 10 μm
λmax ≈ 10 μm — mid infrared
This is why Earth emits IR radiation, not visible light. The Sun (~5778 K) peaks at ~500 nm (visible green). Hotter = shorter wavelength.
Problem 6.2 — Find Temperature from λmax Reverse Calculation

A distant star emits peak thermal radiation at λmax = 580 nm. What is the surface temperature of the star? Is it hotter or cooler than our Sun (T☉ ≈ 5778 K)?

Rearrange Wien's law: T = (2.898×10−3) / λmax. Convert nm → m first!

Step-by-Step Solution

λmax = 580 nm = 5.80 × 10−7 m
T = 2.898×10−3 / 5.80×10−7 = 4,997 K ≈ 5,000 K
5,000 K < 5,778 K → this star is slightly cooler than the Sun.
T ≈ 5,000 K — slightly cooler than the Sun

Ionization Energy Trends

Large jump in IE → just removed a core electron!
Problem 7.1 — Identify the Element Pattern Recognition

An element has the following successive ionization energies. Which element is it?

IonizationEnergy (kJ/mol)
1st578
2nd1,820
3rd2,750
4th11,600
5th14,830

A) Na    B) Mg    C) Al    D) Si

Find where the big jump in ionization energy occurs. The jump happens when you've removed all the valence electrons and start pulling from the filled core shell. Count the ionizations before the jump = number of valence electrons.

Answer: C) Al (Aluminum)

The enormous jump occurs between the 3rd IE (2,750 kJ/mol) and the 4th IE (11,600 kJ/mol).
This means there are 3 valence electrons before hitting the core — placing this element in Group 13.
In Period 3, the Group 13 element is Al (Aluminum): [Ne] 3s² 3p¹
Answer: C) Al — 3 valence electrons, Group 13
Problem 7.2 — Rank Ionization Energies Trend Analysis

Rank the following elements in order of increasing first ionization energy (lowest → highest):  Na, F, Ne, O, N, Li. Explain any exceptions to the general period trend.

General trend: IE increases across a period (left → right) and up a group. But there's a famous exception between N and O — think about N's half-filled 2p³ subshell!

Answer

General trend across Period 2: IE increases left → right (increasing Zeff).
Exception: N > O — Nitrogen has a half-filled 2p³ (one electron in each p orbital, all same spin). This maximizes exchange energy → extra stability → harder to remove an electron from N than from O.
Na and Li: Both Group 1, but Na is in Period 3 (larger, more shielded) so Na < Li.
Full ranking: Na < Li < O < N < F < Ne
Na < Li < O < N < F < Ne
💡 Similar reasoning explains why Be > B (B removes from 2p, easier than Be removing from 2s).

Electron Configuration (& Anomalies)

Aufbau + Pauli + Hund's | Cr & Cu are exceptions!
Problem 8.1 — Write Configurations Direct Practice

Write the full and noble-gas abbreviated electron configurations for: (a) Se (Z=34)   (b) Xe (Z=54)   (c) Sr (Z=38). Also: (d) What is the charge and configuration of the most stable ion of Sr?

Fill in Aufbau order: 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p… Se is in Period 4, Group 16 (6 valence electrons). Sr is in Group 2 — it will lose both s electrons to form its most stable ion.

Answers

(a) Se: Full: 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶3d¹⁰4s²4p⁴  |  Abbreviated: [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p⁴
(b) Xe: Full: 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶3d¹⁰4s²4p⁶4d¹⁰5s²5p⁶  |  Abbreviated: [Kr] 4d¹⁰ 5s² 5p⁶
(c) Sr: [Kr] 5s²
(d) Sr loses both 5s electrons → charge = 2+, configuration = [Kr] (isoelectronic with krypton)
Sr²⁺ with configuration [Kr]
Problem 8.2 — The Anomalies: Cr and Cu ⭐ Common Confusion

(a) Predict the configurations of Cr (Z=24) and Cu (Z=29) using normal Aufbau rules. (b) Write their actual configurations. (c) Explain why these anomalies occur — and how you can recognize an anomaly on an exam.

Count electrons for Cr: you'd normally get 3d⁴ 4s². But the actual config is different! Think about the special stability of exactly half-filled (d⁵) and fully filled (d¹⁰) subshells.

Answers

(a) Predicted (wrong): Cr = [Ar] 3d⁴ 4s²  |  Cu = [Ar] 3d⁹ 4s²
(b) Actual: Cr = [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹  |  Cu = [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹
(c) Why? Half-filled d⁵ and fully-filled d¹⁰ have extra stability from exchange energy. One 4s electron "promotes" to 3d to reach these special configurations.
How to spot anomalies: If an element is 1 electron away from a half-filled or fully-filled d subshell, it is likely an anomaly. For Chem 1X, memorize Cr and Cu!
Cr: [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹  |  Cu: [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹
Other anomalies (for reference): Mo ([Kr] 4d⁵ 5s¹), Ag ([Kr] 4d¹⁰ 5s¹)

de Broglie Wavelength

λ = h / (m · v)
Problem 9.1 — Baseball vs. Electron Calculation

(a) A 0.145 kg baseball moves at 90.0 mph (1 mph = 0.447 m/s). Calculate its de Broglie wavelength in meters. (b) An electron travels at 2.00 × 106 m/s. Calculate its de Broglie wavelength in nm. What does the comparison tell us?
(h = 6.626 × 10−34 J·s, me = 9.109 × 10−31 kg)

λ = h / (m · v)
Convert 90.0 mph to m/s first. For (b), keep units consistent (kg, m/s) and the answer will be in meters — then convert to nm (1 m = 10⁹ nm).

Step-by-Step Solution

(a) v = 90.0 × 0.447 = 40.2 m/s  →  λ = 6.626×10−34 / (0.145 × 40.2) = 6.626×10−34 / 5.83 = 1.14 × 10−34 m
Far too small to observe — baseballs do not have measurable wave behavior!
(b) λ = 6.626×10−34 / (9.109×10−31 × 2.00×106) = 6.626×10−34 / 1.822×10−24 = 3.64×10−10 m = 0.364 nm
0.364 nm is in the X-ray range — electrons really can diffract off crystal lattices!
Baseball: ~10⁻³⁴ m (no wave behavior) | Electron: 0.364 nm (X-ray scale!)
Problem 9.2 — Find Neutron Speed Reverse Calculation

Calculate the speed (in m/s) of a neutron (m = 1.67 × 10−27 kg) that has a de Broglie wavelength of 1.50 Å (angstroms). (1 Å = 1×10−10 m)

Rearrange the de Broglie equation to solve for speed: v = h / (m·λ). Remember to convert Å to meters first.

Step-by-Step Solution

λ = 1.50 Å = 1.50 × 10−10 m
v = h / (m·λ) = 6.626×10−34 / (1.67×10−27 × 1.50×10−10)
= 6.626×10−34 / 2.505×10−37 = 2.64 × 10³ m/s
v ≈ 2.64 × 10³ m/s (these are called "thermal neutrons")

Photon Energy & Wavelength Conversions

E = hν = hc/λ  |  Emol = Nₐ·Ephoton
Problem 10.1 — Four-Way Conversion Practice Multi-part

(a) Find the wavelength (in nm) of light with a molar energy of 2.00 kJ/mol.
(b) Find the frequency (in Hz) of infrared light with a molar energy of 45.0 kJ/mol.
(c) A photon has wavelength 365 nm. Find its energy in J.
(d) Convert your answer in (c) to kJ/mol.

Eper photon = Emol / NA  |  λ = hc/E  |  ν = c/λ  |  NA = 6.022×10²³ mol⁻¹
For (a) and (b): first divide kJ/mol by NA to get J per photon. For (d): multiply J/photon by NA, then divide by 1000 to get kJ/mol.

Step-by-Step Solutions

(a) E/photon = 2000 J/mol ÷ 6.022×10²³ = 3.321×10−21 J  →  λ = 1.988×10−25/3.321×10−21 = 5.99×10−5 m = 59,900 nm (far infrared)
(b) E/photon = 45,000 J/mol ÷ 6.022×10²³ = 7.474×10−20 J  →  ν = E/h = 7.474×10−20/6.626×10−34 = 1.13 × 1014 Hz (infrared ✓)
(c) E = hc/λ = (6.626×10−34 × 3.00×108) / (365×10−9) = 5.45 × 10−19 J
(d) Emol = 5.45×10−19 × 6.022×10²³ = 3.28×105 J/mol = 328 kJ/mol
(a) 59,900 nm  |  (b) 1.13×10¹⁴ Hz  |  (c) 5.45×10⁻¹⁹ J  |  (d) 328 kJ/mol
Problem 10.2 — He⁺ and Li²⁺ Ion Energy Levels Hydrogen-like Ions

(a) Calculate the energy of He⁺ (Z=2) at n=4, in eV and in Joules.   (b) A Li²⁺ ion (Z=3) absorbs a photon of frequency 6.66 × 1014 Hz. If the final state has energy −7.85 × 10−19 J, what is the energy of the initial state?
Use En = −Z² × (13.598 eV) / n².

For (b): when a photon is absorbed, the ion gains energy: Efinal = Einitial + Ephoton. Rearrange to solve for Einitial = Efinal − Ephoton.

Step-by-Step Solutions

(a) He⁺: Z=2, n=4  →  E4 = −(2²)(13.598)/4² = −(4 × 13.598)/16 = −3.40 eV
In Joules: −3.40 × 1.602×10−19 = −5.45 × 10−19 J
(b) Ephoton = hν = 6.626×10−34 × 6.66×1014 = 4.41 × 10−19 J
Einitial = Efinal − Ephoton = −7.85×10−19 − 4.41×10−19 = −1.23 × 10−18 J
(a) −3.40 eV / −5.45×10⁻¹⁹ J  |  (b) Einitial = −1.23 × 10⁻¹⁸ J

Stoichiometry & Mole Conversions

mol = g/M  |  N = mol × Nₐ
Problem 11.1 — The Mole Map Fundamental

(a) How many formula units are in 58.44 g of NaCl? (M = 58.44 g/mol)  
(b) What is the mass of 3.01 × 10²³ atoms of carbon? (MC = 12.01 g/mol)  
(c) How many moles is 2.50 g of water? (MH₂O = 18.02 g/mol)

grams → moles: divide by M (g/mol)  |  moles → particles: multiply by 6.022×10²³
Think of the mole map: Grams ÷ Molar Mass = Moles. Moles × Avogadro's Number = Particles. These two conversions are all you need!

Step-by-Step Solutions

(a) mol NaCl = 58.44 / 58.44 = 1.000 mol  →  units = 1.000 × 6.022×10²³ = 6.022 × 10²³ formula units
(b) mol C = 3.01×10²³ / 6.022×10²³ = 0.4999 mol  →  mass = 0.500 × 12.01 = 6.01 g
(c) mol H₂O = 2.50 / 18.02 = 0.139 mol
(a) 6.022×10²³ units  |  (b) 6.01 g  |  (c) 0.139 mol
Problem 11.2 — Energy Per Mole of Photons Connecting Stoich to Quantum

A chemical bond absorbs near-infrared light at 800 nm. (a) What is the energy per photon in J? (b) What is this energy per mole of photons in kJ/mol?

Find E per photon using E = hc/λ. Then multiply by NA = 6.022×10²³ to get J/mol, then divide by 1000 to get kJ/mol.

Step-by-Step Solution

λ = 800 nm = 8.00 × 10−7 m
Ephoton = hc/λ = (6.626×10−34 × 3.00×108) / 8.00×10−7 = 2.485 × 10−19 J
Emol = 2.485×10−19 × 6.022×10²³ = 1.496×105 J/mol = 149.6 kJ/mol ≈ 150 kJ/mol
Ephoton ≈ 2.49 × 10⁻¹⁹ J  |  Emol ≈ 150 kJ/mol

Coulomb's Law & Potential Energy

PE = k·q₁·q₂/r  (more negative = lower = more stable)
Problem 12.1 — Rank the Ion Pairs Conceptual

Rank the following ion pairs from lowest to highest potential energy (most stable → least stable):

PairChargesDistance
A−1 and +3100 pm
B−2 and +2120 pm
C−1 and +2150 pm
D−1 and +1200 pm
PE ∝ (q₁ × q₂) / r. Calculate a relative value for each pair — most negative value = lowest PE = most stable. You don't need to use actual constants; just compare the ratios!

Step-by-Step Solution

Relative PE ∝ (q₁ × q₂) / r:
A: (−1)(+3) / 100 = −3/100 = −0.0300
B: (−2)(+2) / 120 = −4/120 = −0.0333 ← most negative
C: (−1)(+2) / 150 = −2/150 = −0.0133
D: (−1)(+1) / 200 = −1/200 = −0.0050
Order lowest → highest PE: B < A < C < D
Lowest → Highest PE: B < A < C < D (B is most stable)
💡 Higher charge magnitude and shorter distance both lower PE. Pair B wins because of its larger charge product, even though its distance is larger than A's.

Bonus: Wave-Particle Duality & Double-Slit

Observation destroys interference!
Problem B.1 — The Double-Slit Experiment Multiple Choice

Electrons pass through two slits under two conditions: (1) sent one at a time with no detector at the slits; (2) a detector records which slit each electron passes through. What pattern builds up on the screen?

A) Interference pattern in both conditions
B) No interference in either condition
C) Interference in (1), but not in (2)
D) Two bright bands in (1), but interference in (2)

Ask yourself: does observing the electron change its behavior? If you know which path it took, can it still have interfered with itself?

Answer: C

Condition (1) — No detector: Each electron acts as a wave and passes through both slits simultaneously, interfering with itself. An interference pattern builds up — even when sent one at a time!
Condition (2) — With detector: Measuring which slit the electron went through forces it to behave as a particle through one slit. The interference pattern disappears — replaced by two bright bands (like bullets).
The act of measurement itself changes the outcome. This is the heart of quantum mechanics!
Answer: C — Interference only when the path is unobserved
This experiment has been done with single electrons, single photons, and even large molecules — it always gives the same result. Observation collapses the wavefunction.
